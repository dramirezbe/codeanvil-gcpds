## Abstract

Infrared unmanned aerial vehicle detection is essential for low altitude security and surveillance. However, infrared UAVs typically exhibit small sizes, weak textures, and low contrast, making them easily confused with target like clutter in complex backgrounds. Repeated spatial downsampling in existing detectors progressively weakens discriminative target responses, while direct multiscale fusion introduces additional interference because of the semantic gap between shallow details and deep representations. To address these challenges, we propose SRCG, a singleframe infrared UAV detector that preserves target background separability during feature extraction and improves semantic consistency during multiscale fusion. SRCG introduces a Spatial Frequency Representation Block that combines a spatial branch with a wavelet based frequency branch and adaptively integrates their complementary representations through a Spatial Frequency Interaction Module. We further develop a Cross Guide Gate that uses deep semantic information to selectively refine shallow features, suppress background clutter, and retain target relevant details before feature fusion. Extensive experiments on the Anti-UAV410 and Anti-UAV datasets demonstrate that SRCG achieves the best mAP-S and mAP-50 among the compared singleframe and multiframe methods, while attaining the highest F1 score on Anti- UAV410 and maintaining real time inference at 101 FPS. The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/AmazingDay1/SRCG.

# 1. Introduction

Compact unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been widely deployed in civilian and public applications (Xu et al., 2025). However, their unauthorized use has introduced growing threats to low altitude security (Qi et al., 2026; Zhuang et al., 2025). Accurate and timely UAV detection has therefore become an important requirement for modern surveillance systems (Zhang et al., 2023). Among different sensing modalities, infrared imaging is particularly suitable for continuous UAV monitoring because it provides stable thermal responses under adverse weather and is less sensitive to illumination variations (Fang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, compact UAVs in low altitude infrared scenes usually occupy only a few pixels and exhibit weak textures, low contrast, substantial scale variation, and complex background interference. These characteristics make them difficult to distinguish from surrounding clutter (Yu et al., 2026; Zuo et al., 2025). Existing infrared detection methods can be broadly divided into singleframe spatial detection and multi frame temporal detection. Early singleframe methods are mainly model driven and rely on manually designed saliency features (Zhao et al., 2022a; Han et al., 2019), spatial filtering operators (Bai and Zhou, 2010), and low rank or prior based formulations (Zhang et al., 2018). Although these methods are lightweight and interpretable, their effectiveness often depends on ideal assumptions such as background smoothness and target sparsity. Such assumptions are frequently violated in practical low altitude scenes, where buildings, ∗Corresponding author guilixu@nuaa.edu.cn (G. Xu) forests, roads, and clouds may generate locally salient responses that resemble small UAVs. Consequently, these methods are sensitive to complex clutter and often produce missed detections or false alarms. To reduce the dependence on manually designed priors, recent data driven methods learn discriminative representations directly from infrared observations. Because small UAVs contain limited pixels and weak structural information, several methods incorporate auxiliary cues into deep networks, including residual mapping (Fang et al., 2021), gradient priors (Yang et al., 2022), and high order derivatives (Zhao et al., 2023). Other approaches improve small target representation through multiscale feature reuse, interference suppression, or prior guided modeling (Lu et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2026). Frequency information has also been introduced because the boundaries and local structures of small targets are closely related to high frequency components. HS-FPN (Shi et al., 2025) extracts high frequency responses and uses them to reweight feature pyramid representations. FM-Net (Liu et al., 2025) employs wavelet residual blocks and frequency modulation attention to emphasize informative frequency components. ARFC-WAHNet (Cui et al., 2025) introduces a Haar wavelet module during downsampling and uses the resulting frequency responses to enhance spatial features. Despite their effectiveness, these methods mainly treat frequency information as an auxiliary enhancement. They attempt to recover or strengthen target cues after these cues have already been attenuated during feature extraction. Another line of research exploits temporal information from consecutive frames. Multi frame detectors compensate for weak singleframe observations by modeling temporal 4× 8× temporal representations (Peng et al., 2025), introducing additional frequency branches (Duan et al., 2024), or reformulating detection as anomaly discovery (Duan et al., 2026). Although temporal cues can improve the detection of dim targets, these methods often require complex parallel branches and incur additional computation and inference latency. More importantly, their performance depends on clean and continuous image sequences. Occlusion, camera jitter, abrupt motion, and frame loss can disrupt temporal consistency and reduce their reliability in practical surveillance systems. Both frequency enhancement and temporal modeling mainly compensate for weak target responses after degradation, while the downsampling process that causes this degradation remains underexplored. As shown in Fig. 1(a), repeated spatial downsampling can suppress weak UAV responses when stronger target like clutter occurs in the same local region, progressively reducing the signal to clutter ratio and entangling target cues with background responses. To address this issue, we propose SRCG, a singleframe infrared UAV detector that preserves target and background separability during feature extraction. Its Spatial Frequency Representation Block introduces a Discrete Wavelet Transform branch parallel to the spatial branch at each downsampling stage, retaining weak high frequency structures through frequency decomposition rather than discarding them through pooling or strided convolution (Finder et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1(b), this frequency pathway maintains a more stable signal to clutter ratio at deeper stages. Directly combining spatial and frequency features is nevertheless ineffective because the two representations exhibit different response distributions. Dense frequency responses may introduce background edges and overwhelm the compact semantic information carried by the spatial branch. We therefore design a Spatial Frequency Interaction Module (SIM), which enriches spatial context at multiple receptive fields and adaptively recalibrates the combined features. This design assigns higher priority to spatial semantics while using frequency information to supplement weak target boundaries. In addition, shallow features contain detailed target structures but also substantial background noise, whereas deep features provide stronger semantics with reduced spatial precision. To address this mismatch, we introduce a Cross Guide Gate (CGG), which uses deep semantic information to selectively refine shallow features before multiscale fusion. The resulting representation preserves target relevant details, suppresses background interference, and reduces the semantic gap across network stages. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose SRCG, a singleframe infrared UAV detector that preserves target background separability during downsampling and improves semantic consistency during multiscale fusion. • We design the Spatial Frequency Representation Block with parallel a wavelet based frequency and spatial downsampling, and a Spatial Frequency Interaction Module for effective spatial frequency fusion. • We introduce the Cross Guide Gate to refine shallow features with deep semantics, suppress background interference, and retain target relevant details. • Extensive experiments on Anti-UAV410 and Anti- UAV show that SRCG achieves the best mAP𝑆 and mAP50 while maintaining real time inference.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1. Infrared Object Detection

Infrared object detection methods can be broadly divided into singleframe spatial methods and multi frame spatio temporal methods. Early singleframe approaches mainly rely on manually designed priors. Bai and Zhou (Bai and Zhou, 2010) developed a top hat transformation to enhance local target contrast and suppress clutter. Han et al. (Han et al., 2019) introduced Gaussian filtering before contrast measurement to strengthen weak target responses. Zhao et al. (Zhao et al., 2022a) proposed a local uncertainty measure based on component consistency, while Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2018) combined a nonconvex rank surrogate with a structured 𝑙2,1 norm for background suppression. Although these methods are interpretable and computationally efficient, they rely heavily on assumptions about target sparsity and background smoothness. Such assumptions are often violated in low altitude scenes containing buildings, forests, clouds, and other target like clutter. Data driven methods learn discriminative representations directly from infrared observations (Zhao et al., 2022b). To improve weak target representation, several studies introduce residual mapping (Fang et al., 2021), gradient priors (Yang et al., 2022), or high order derivatives (Zhao et al., 2023). Other methods enhance multiscale interaction or suppress thermal distractors through feature pyramid design and prior guided modeling (Lu et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2026). However, these approaches mainly strengthen target responses after backbone feature extraction and do not explicitly prevent the loss of fine target structures during downsampling. Multi frame methods instead exploit temporal information to compensate for limited singleframe evidence. Existing approaches model low rank temporal correlations (Zhu et al., 2019), spatial appearance and motion cues (Zhu et al., 2024), adaptive spatial and temporal interaction (Peng et al., 2025), frequency assisted motion representation (Duan et al., 2024), or recurrent spatio temporal features (Chen et al., 2024a). CHAL (Duan et al., 2026) further formulates detection as background centered anomaly discovery. Although effective, these methods usually require complex temporal modeling and continuous image sequences, which increase inference cost and reduce robustness under occlusion, camera jitter, and frame loss. In contrast, SRCG improves small UAV representation using only a single infrared frame.

## 2.2. Frequency Modeling in Deep Learning

Frequency modeling provides an effective way to separate image structures across different spectral components. Wavelet transforms are particularly suitable for hierarchical representation because they support multiresolution analysis, spatial frequency localization, and invertible decomposition. Guth et al. (Guth et al., 2022) introduced wavelet representations into score based generative modeling to improve efficiency and generation quality. Finder et al. (Finder et al., 2022) used wavelet transforms to compress feature maps and reduce the computational cost of convolutional networks. WCNN3D (Alaba and Ball, 2022) replaced conventional pooling with wavelet decomposition for three dimensional object detection. More broadly, recent diffusion models have demonstrated that explicit priors and structured conditioning can improve controllability in garment generation, pose guided synthesis, virtual dressing, and long term video generation (Shen et al., 2025c; Shen and Tang, 2024; Shen et al., 2025a, 2024, 2025b). These studies further support the importance of introducing structured representations into visual learning systems. Frequency cues have also been explored for small target representation. HS-FPN (Shi et al., 2025) extracts high frequency responses and uses them to reweight spatial and channel features in a feature pyramid. FSGNet (Zhang et al., 2026) applies Fourier based frequency modeling to suppress clutter in skip connections. FM-Net (Liu et al., 2025) combines wavelet residual blocks with frequency modulation attention, while ARFC-WAHNet (Cui et al., 2025) introduces Haar wavelet decomposition to enhance target responses during downsampling. SWAN (Jing et al., 2025) further integrates wavelet convolution with attention based background suppression. Despite their effectiveness, most existing methods use frequency information as auxiliary attention, feature filtering, or response enhancement. SRCG instead preserves frequency information through a parallel wavelet branch at each downsampling stage. The Spatial Frequency Interaction Module fuses spatial and frequency features, while the Cross Guide Gate refines shallow features with deep semantics.

# 3. Method

## 3.1. Overview

We propose the Spatial Frequency Representation and Cross-guide Gating (SRCG) model to address the loss of target background separability caused by spatial downsampling in infrared UAV detection and to bridge the semantic gap between shallow and deep representations. Unlike conventional detectors that rely solely on spatial downsampling, SRCG adopts a multi stage parallel spatial frequency extraction architecture, as illustrated in Fig.2. SRCG first processes the input tensor 𝐼 through a stem block to extract initial lowlevel features, reduce feature size, and expand the channel dimensions, generating a preliminary feature map 𝑋. Then, the features 𝑋 are sequentially fed into stacked four stage Spatial Frequency Representation Blocks (SRBs), which employ a hierarchical downsampling strategy, progressively reducing resolutions from 𝑊 ∕4 × 𝐻∕4 to 𝑊 ∕32 × 𝐻∕32. Each SRB consists of a parallel spatial feature extractor and a frequency feature extractor, integrated via a Spatial Frequency Interaction Module (SIM), so that target background separability is maintained throughout downsampling. The output of the last SRB is further processed by an SPPC- SPC (Wang et al., 2023) block to bolster UAV salient features. In addition, we construct a feature pyramid network to fuse multi-scale features to handle the scale variations of UAVs. Meanwhile, to coordinate shallow details with deep semantics, we design the Cross Guide Gate (CGG), in which the adaptively refined shallow features are concatenated and fused with the deep semantics. Finally, the fused features are forwarded to the detection head for final bounding box prediction.

## 3.2. Spatial Frequency Representation Blocks

Existing infrared UAV detection relies on spatial convolution and pooling for feature extraction and downsampling. For infrared small UAVs, repeated spatial downsampling progressively erodes target background separability. The max-pooling retains only the strongest local activation, so the weak response of a small UAV is easily discarded once a stronger target like background response appears in the same window. And the strided convolution performing fixed subsampling is similarly prone to attenuating such weak cues. As the network deepens, these losses accumulate and the target gradually becomes entangled with the clutter. Since the discriminative cues of small UAVs reside largely in high-frequency components (Shi et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), this motivates a downsampling scheme that explicitly decouples and retains high frequency information rather than discarding it. To this end, we propose the Spatial Frequency Representation Block (SRB) as the core building block of our network. ## 3.2.1. Spatial Feature Extractor

To capture local spatial correlations and salient responses, we combine a convolution branch and a maxpooling branch in parallel as a spatial feature extractor to perform spatial downsampling. As shown in Fig.2, given an input feature 𝑋 ∈ ℝ𝐶×𝐻×𝑊 , the convolution branch utilizes a 1 × 1 convolution followed by a 3 × 3 convolution with a stride of 2 to downsample and capture local spatial correlations. The max-pooling branch employs a 2 × 2 kernel for downsampling and a 1 × 1 convolution to adjust the channel dimension. Formally, the spatial downsampling process is defined as: 𝑋𝑚 = Conv1×1(MaxPool(𝑋)), 𝑋𝑐 = Conv3×3(Conv1×1(𝑋)), 𝑋𝑡 = Concat(𝑋𝑐, 𝑋𝑚), (1) where 𝑋𝑚, 𝑋𝑐 ∈ ℝ𝐶∕2×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 denote the outputs of the max pooling and convolution branches, and their concatenation yields the aggregated spatial feature 𝑋𝑡 ∈ ℝ𝐶×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2. By concatenating the outputs of both branches along the channel dimension, the spatial feature extractor achieves effective spatial downsampling while preserving robust spatial semantics as the structural foundation for subsequent fusion.

## 3.2.2. Frequency Feature Extractor

Although the spatial feature extractor captures robust semantics, the weak boundaries and fine textures of small UAVs tend to be attenuated during spatial downsampling. Therefore, we design the frequency feature extractor to complement these details. The frequency feature extractor leverages the 2 dimensional Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) for downsampling. Unlike pooling operations that discard high frequency responses, the DWT explicitly decouples rather than discards them: the four subbands jointly reorganize the input into separate frequency components, deferring the selection of informative cues to the subsequent learnable fusion. As shown in Fig.2, given the same feature 𝑋 ∈ ℝ𝐶×𝐻×𝑊 , the DWT separates it into four subbands via lowpass and high-pass filters: [𝑋𝐿𝐿, 𝑋𝐿𝐻 , 𝑋𝐻𝐿, 𝑋𝐻𝐻 ] = DWT(𝑋), (2) where 𝑋𝐿𝐿 ∈ ℝ𝐶×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 is the low-frequency approximation that captures the fundamental semantic structure, while 𝑋𝐿𝐻 , 𝑋𝐻𝐿, and 𝑋𝐻𝐻 ∈ ℝ𝐶×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 encode the high-frequency details along the horizontal, vertical, and diagonal directions, respectively. These high frequency components carry the fine boundaries and contours that distinguish infrared UAVs from complex backgrounds. In our implementation, we adopt the Biorthogonal 1.3 wavelet (Cohen et al., 1992) from the Cohen-Daubechies Feauveau family. The choice is motivated by two complementary requirements for preserving the fine structures of small UAVs. First, the filters should be symmetric so that edge positions are not shifted during decomposition. Higher-order Daubechies wavelets are asymmetric and introduce phase distortion that misaligns fine target boundaries. Second, the filters should be sufficiently long and smooth to provide adequate frequency selectivity; the Haar wavelet, although linear phase, has the shortest and discontinuous filters, and tends to introduce blocking artifacts on smooth edges. Among common wavelet bases, biorthogonal wavelets relax the orthogonality constraint to satisfy both requirements simultaneously: Biorthogonal 1.3 combines symmetric, linear phase filters with a longer, smoother analysis filter, thereby preserving the weak, symmetric edge structures of small UAVs during decomposition. A quantitative comparison of these wavelet bases is provided in Sec.4.5 and Table 9, which confirms that Biorthogonal 1.3 yields the best detection performance. To integrate the decomposed subbands, we first concatenate them along the channel dimension and then apply a pointwise convolution to adaptively fuse and reweight the distinct frequency components, forming a comprehensive frequency representation: 𝑋𝑏 = Concat(𝑋𝐿𝐿, 𝑋𝐿𝐻 , 𝑋𝐻𝐿, 𝑋𝐻𝐻 ), 𝑋𝑤 = Conv1×1(𝑋𝑏), (3) where 𝑋𝑤 ∈ ℝ𝐶∕2×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 denotes the frequency feature. The pointwise convolution simultaneously fuses the four sub-bands and reduces the concatenated 4𝐶 channels to 𝐶∕2, so that the frequency pathway supplements the spatial one at a compact cost. Through this wavelet pathway, the frequency feature extractor decouples and retains the fine grained details that spatial downsampling tends to attenuate, thereby complementing the spatial branch and enhancing target background separability.

## 3.2.3. Spatial Frequency Interaction Module

After obtaining the spatial feature 𝑋𝑡 and the frequency feature 𝑋𝑤, an effective fusion mechanism is required. However, the two originate from heterogeneous domains, and directly fusing them is suboptimal. The dense frequency responses, in which target contours and background clutter remain entangled, tend to overwhelm the compact spatial semantics that anchor the target representation. As verified in Sec 4.5, Table 6, such naive fusion even underperforms the spatial branch alone. To this end, we design the Spatial Frequency Interaction Module (SIM), which first enriches the spatial pathway with multiscale receptive fields and then adaptively integrates it with the frequency feature in a priority aware manner. As shown in Fig.2, before cross domain fusion, we first enrich the spatial feature 𝑋𝑡 with multiple receptive fields to strengthen its contextual representation, so that the spatial semantics remain dominant in the subsequent fusion. Specifically, 𝑋𝑡 is processed by a lightweight parallel architecture to obtain 1 × 1, 3 × 3, and 5 × 5 receptive field features 𝑋1, 𝑋3, and 𝑋5: 𝑋1 = Conv1×1(𝑋𝑡), 𝑋3 = Conv3×3(𝑋𝑡), 𝑋5 = Conv3×3(𝑋3), (4) where 𝑋5 serves as a deeper bottleneck branch that stacks two 3 × 3 convolutions to obtain a larger effective receptive field, capturing broader spatial context without the heavy computation of a single large kernel. Subsequently, we concatenate these multiscale spatial features with the frequency feature 𝑋𝑤 along the channel dimension: 𝑋𝑓 = Concat(𝑋1, 𝑋3, 𝑋5, 𝑋𝑤), (5) where 𝑋1, 𝑋3, 𝑋5 ∈ ℝ𝐶∕2×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 are the multiscale spatial features and 𝑋𝑓 ∈ ℝ2𝐶×𝐻∕2×𝑊 ∕2 denotes the concatenated spatial /frequency feature. Since these features come from different domains and receptive fields, we adopt a Channel Attention (Hu et al., 2018) to adaptively recalibrate their channel weights. Specifically, 𝑋𝑓 is first processed by a Global Average Pooling layer (GAP) to aggregate channel wise context. A 1 × 1 convolution then reduces the channel dimensionality to model channel correlation, followed by a ReLU activation. To restore the channel dimension and capture cross channel spatial frequency interactions, another 1×1 convolution is applied, and a sigmoid function generates the attention weights. These weights are multiplied with 𝑋𝑓 element wise to produce the refined output: 𝑊 = 𝜎(Conv1×1(𝛿(Conv1×1(GAP(𝑋𝑓 ))))), 𝑋𝑜 = 𝑋𝑓 ⊗ 𝑊 , (6) where 𝛿 and 𝜎 denote the ReLU and Sigmoid functions, ⊗ represents element wise multiplication, and 𝑋𝑜 denotes the fused spatial frequency feature. The SIM achieves priority aware fusion in two aspects. First, the multiscale enrichment keeps the spatial semantics dominant in the concatenated Conv1*1 DWConv 3*3 Conv1*1 Conv3*3 Element c c Xc Xr multiplication feature: the frequency feature occupies only one quarter of the channels in 𝑋𝑓 , while the three multiscale spatial features account for the remaining three quarters, so that the frequency cues enter the fusion as a supplement rather than a competing representation. Second, the channel attention weighting in Eq.(6) adaptively assigns higher weights to channels carrying target boundaries while suppressing redundant frequency responses. In this way, the frequency features supplement the fine details attenuated by spatial downsampling without contaminating the spatial semantics, thereby improving target background separability.

## 3.3. Cross Guide Gate

In multiscale fusion, shallow features contain rich spatial details but weak semantics, whereas deep features possess strong semantics but lack finegrained details. Fusing them directly via element wise addition or simple concatenation ignores the inherent semantic gap between stages: the low level noise in shallow features contaminates the deep semantics, while the smooth deep responses blur critical low level contours. To address this issue, we propose the Cross Guide Gate (CGG). As shown in Fig.3, CGG leverages deep features to adaptively filter and refine shallow features before multiscale fusion. Let 𝑋𝑠 ∈ ℝ𝐶1×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8 denote the highresolution shallow feature, and 𝑋𝑑 ∈ ℝ𝐶2×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8 denote the spatially aligned upsampled deep feature. The CGG takes these two hierarchical representations as inputs to formulate a cross level gating mechanism. First, to establish a comprehensive context 𝑋𝑐 that encompasses both finegrained details and global semantics, we concatenate the two feature maps along the channel dimension: 𝑋𝑐 = Concat(𝑋𝑠, 𝑋𝑑), (7) where 𝑋𝑐 ∈ ℝ(𝐶1+𝐶2)×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8. Subsequently, 𝑋𝑐 is fed into a gate generation network. To model the guiding relationship between the spatial details of shallow features and the semantics of deep features, 𝑋𝑐 is first processed by a

1 × 1 convolution for cross channel information fusion and

dimensionality reduction. A 3 × 3 Depthwise Convolution (DWConv) then spatially aggregates local context so that the generated gate preserves target boundaries and suppresses surrounding noise, followed by a ReLU activation and another 1 × 1 convolution. The gating weights are finally normalized into the (0, 1) range by a Sigmoid function: 𝐺 = 𝜎(Conv1×1(𝛿(DWConv3×3(Conv1×1(𝑋𝑐))))), (8) where 𝛿 and 𝜎 denote the ReLU and Sigmoid functions, and 𝐺 ∈ ℝ𝐶1×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8 is the generated gate. The gate 𝐺 then modulates the shallow feature via element-wise multiplication: 𝑋𝑟 = 𝑋𝑠 ⊗ 𝐺, (9) where 𝑋𝑟 ∈ ℝ𝐶1×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8 denotes the refined shallow feature. Acting as a soft mask guided by deep semantics, this operation selectively retains target relevant structural details while suppressing background responses. Finally, the refined shallow feature is concatenated with the original deep feature, and a 3 × 3 convolution integrates these hierarchical features and eliminates potential aliasing effects to produce the robust multiscale fusion: 𝑋𝑝 = Conv3×3(Concat(𝑋𝑟, 𝑋𝑑)), (10) where 𝑋𝑝 ∈ ℝ𝐶2×𝐻∕8×𝑊 ∕8 denotes the fused multiscale feature. By introducing the CGG, SRCG adaptively refines shallow features and bridges the semantic gap across network stages, providing a semantically consistent multiscale representation for the detection head.

## 4.1.2. Implementation Details

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. To strictly verify the effectiveness of our model, we did not employ any complex data augmentation. The input images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels and formatted as 3 channel grayscale images. We adopt the standard detection loss of YOLOv7 (Wang et al., 2023), combining bounding box regression, objectness, and classification terms. For optimization, the batch size was set to 8, and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) was used with an initial learning rate of 1×10−2, a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 5 × 10−4. A cosine annealing strategy with a warmup period was adopted for learning rate scheduling, and the network was trained for 100 epochs.

## 4.2. Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate our model in terms of both detection accuracy and computational efficiency, we report Precision (P), Recall (R), F1 score (F1), and mean Average Precision (mAP) for accuracy, together with the number of parameters (Params), Giga Floating point Operations (GFLOPs), and Frames Per Second (FPS) for efficiency. Precision measures the proportion of true targets among all positive predictions, reflecting the ability to suppress background interference and reduce false alarms, while recall measures the proportion of groundtruth targets that are successfully detected, reflecting the ability to reduce missed detections. ## 4.5. Ablation Study and Analysis

Effectiveness of SRB and CGG. To systematically validate the contribution of each proposed module, we conduct ablation studies as reported in Table 3. Starting from the original YOLOv7 (the first row), we first remove its heavy multibranch PANet (Wang et al., 2023) and replace it with a lightweight vanilla feature pyramid network (He et al., 2015) using simple concatenation for multiscale fusion (denoted as w/o PANet, the second row). This simplification reduces the model weight while slightly improving mAP50 from 86.4% to 86.9%, indicating that the heavy PANet is not essential for this task and providing a clean basis upon which our modules are evaluated. We then progressively integrate the Spatial Frequency Representation Block (SRB) and the Cross Guide Gate (CGG). Adding the SRB alone improves the w/o PANet model across all metrics, raising mAP50 from 86.9% to 87.5% and, more importantly, mAP𝑆 from 37.5% to 38.6%. This is consistent with our design in Sec. 3.2: by introducing a parallel wavelet based frequency pathway, the SRB decouples high and low frequency components during downsampling and better maintains the separability of small UAVs that are otherwise entangled with clutter. To further quantify how the two downsampling schemes affect target background separability, we compute the mean Signal to Clutter Ratio (SCR) of the target region on the spatial and frequency branch features across all four stages, averaged over the small-target samples of Anti-UAV410. Specifically, the SCR is defined as |𝜇𝑡 − 𝜇𝑏| 𝜎𝑏 , (14) where 𝜇𝑡 denotes the mean response within the groundtruth target box on the channel wise energy map of the branch feature (upsampled to the input resolution), and 𝜇𝑏 and 𝜎𝑏 denote the mean and standard deviation of the background region, defined as a 3× extension of the target box excluding the target itself. The reported values are averaged over all small target samples in the Anti-UAV410 test set. As reported in Table 5, a clear trend emerges: the SCR of the spatial branch degrades sharply as downsampling deepens, dropping from 5.42 at stage 1 to 2.53 at stage 4 with a reduction of over 50%, which quantitatively verifies the progressive separability erosion illustrated in Fig. 1. In contrast, the frequency branch maintains a much more stable SCR, declining only mildly from 4.14 to 3.50, and surpasses the spatial branch from stage 2 onward, with the gap widening at deeper stages, with 3.50 in the frequency branch and 2.53 in the spatial branch at stage 4. Notably, at stage 1, the spatial branch still exhibits a higher SCR, since the target response remains strong under mild downsampling. The advantage of the frequency branch emerges precisely where it is needed, in the deeper stages where spatial downsampling has severely entangled the target with clutter. This stage wise evidence explains why the SRB, which injects the frequency pathway at every downsampling stage, is particularly effective for preserving small targets. Adding the CGG alone raises mAP𝑆 from 37.5% to 38.5% while keeping mAP50 essentially unchanged, accompanied by a marginal decrease in F1 from 93.87% to 93.73%. This behavior is consistent with the mechanism described in Sec. 3.3: the gate is generated from joint shallow detail and deep semantic context that suppresses shallow level background noise, which selectively benefits small targets whose finegrained details are most vulnerable to being blurred during direct concatenation. However, when applied to the baseline shallow features, in which weak target responses are already entangled with clutter after spatial downsampling, the gate occasionally suppresses faint true target responses along with the background, slightly reducing recall at the fixed operating point. This limitation is precisely resolved once the SRB is incorporated: with separability preserved shallow features as input, the gating becomes more discriminative, and the full model achieves the best results across all metrics, confirming that the two modules are complementary rather than independent additive. When both modules are combined, the full SRCG achieves the best results, with mAP𝑆 reaching 40.0%, mAP50 87.9%, and F1 94.49%. Notably, the mAP𝑆 of the full model with 40.0% clearly exceeds those of the w/o PANet model and of either module alone, indicating a complementary effect between the two modules on small target detection. This is because the two modules address separability at different stages: the SRB preserves discriminative details during downsampling and feeds high quality shallow features forward, while the CGG ensures that these details are selectively retained rather than blurred during late stage multiscale fusion. Their combination thus yields a consistent improvement, particularly on the challenging small-UAV metric mAP𝑆. Beyond accuracy, we also examine the computational cost, as reported in Table 4. Compared with the original YOLOv7 with 104.7 GFLOPs, 36.9M parameters, 118 FPS, the full SRCG requires fewer computations with 96.7 GFLOPs and fewer parameters with 32M while still running in real time at 101 FPS on a single RTX 4090. This is because the removal of the heavy PANet offsets the lightweight cost of the SRB and CGG, and the DWT itself is parameter free. These results indicate that SRCG reduces parameters and theoretical FLOPs relative to YOLOv7 and still maintains realtime inference and accuracy gains, particularly the 2.5% improvement in mAP𝑆, although its measured FPS is lower because of the wavelet and multibranch operations. Analysis of the SRB. To understand the internal mechanism of the SRB, we ablate its constituent elements: the spatial feature extractor (Spatial), the frequency feature extractor (Frequency), and the fusion strategy, including simple concatenation (Concat) versus our Spatial Frequency Interaction Module (SIM). The results are presented in Table 6. Using the Spatial branch alone yields an mAP50 of 87.2% and an mAP𝑆 of 38.6%, while using the Frequency branch alone performs slightly worse. It is expected that frequency details alone lack the semantic structure needed to localize targets, so neither single domain branch is sufficient on its own. A critical phenomenon appears in the third row: directly integrating the spatial and frequency features by simple concatenation degrades performance relative to the Spatial branch alone, with mAP50 dropping from 87.2% to 86.6% and mAP𝑆 from 38.6% to 37.7%. This validates the two aspects of the SIM design. First, it enriches the spatial pathway with multiscale receptive fields so that the spatial semantics remain dominant. Second, through the channel attention weighting, it adaptively emphasizes channels carrying target boundaries while suppressing redundant frequency responses. A potential concern is whether the DWT genuinely exploits its high frequency subbands or merely acts as a fixed, parameter free downsampler. To clarify this, we ablate the frequency subbands used by the frequency feature extractor, as reported in Table 8. When only the low frequency approximation is retained, which reduces the DWT to a low pass downsampler, the performance drops to 38.1% mAP𝑆 and 87.0% mAP50, even below the full model by 1.9% and 0.9%. This asymmetry is informative: the low frequency semantic structure is largely redundant with the parallel spatial branch, whereas the high frequency subbands carry the majority of the complementary information that the spatial pathway lacks. Only when all four subbands are jointly employed does the model reach its best performance. These results confirm that the frequency branch works primarily through its high frequency components rather than acting as a plain low pass downsampler. Effect of Different Wavelet Bases We further examine the choice of wavelet basis, as reported in Table 9. We compare the adopted Biorthogonal 1.3 wavelet with two widely used orthogonal wavelets, Haar and Daubechies. The Biorthogonal 1.3 wavelet achieves the best results across all metrics with 40.0% mAP𝑆, 87.9% mAP50, and 94.49% F1, consistently outperforming Haar and Daubechies on mAP𝑆. Notably, Haar and Daubechies incur comparable degradations on mAP𝑆, despite stemming from distinct causes: the blocking artifacts of the discontinuous Haar filters and the phase distortion of the asymmetric Daubechies filters, respectively. This symmetry in performance loss suggests that both edgesmoothness and phase alignment are indispensable for preserving the fragile boundary structures of small UAVs, and that violating either one is similarly harmful. 1.3 is the only basis among the three that circumvents both

defects, combining symmetric, linear phase filters with a longer, smoother analysis filter, which is consistent with the two complementary requirements analyzed in Sec. 3.2. Since all three bases share the same decomposition structure, this improvement is obtained without any additional computational overhead, justifying our choice of Biorthogonal 1.3.

# 5. Conclusion

We presented SRCG, a singleframe infrared UAV detection model that addresses the loss of target background separability during downsampling and the semantic gap in multiscale fusion. Instead of compensating for weak targets after feature extraction, SRCG integrates a Spatial Frequency Representation Block (SRB) that couples a spatial branch with a parallel wavelet based frequency branch, and uses a Spatial Frequency Interaction Module (SIM) to fuse them in a priority aware manner, mitigating the degradation of target background separability. A Cross Guide Gate (CGG) further leverages deep semantics to adaptively refine shallow features, alleviating the semantic gap during multiscale fusion. Extensive experiments on the Anti-UAV410 and Anti-UAV datasets show that SRCG achieves the best small target accuracy among both singleframe and multiframe competitors while running in real time. In future work, we will explore extending the spatial frequency representation to more efficient and lightweight architectures for onboard UAV surveillance.
